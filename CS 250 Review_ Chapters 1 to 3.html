    1.1 Introduction

    Opening quote: Alfred North Whitehead, An Introduction to Mathematics, 1911: civilization advances by extending the number of important operations we can perform without thinking about them.

    Why computing is a revolution

    • The information technology industry is responsible for almost 10% of US gross national product, and the US economy partly depends on its rapid improvements.
    • In the last 40 years, several new computers seemed to revolutionize the industry, and each revolution ended only because someone built a better computer.
    • Electronic computing began in the late 1940s.
    • If transportation had kept pace with computing, you could travel New York to London in a second for a penny. The book imagines living in Tahiti while working in San Francisco, or going to Moscow for an evening at the Bolshoi Ballet.
    • Computing is the third revolution for civilization, alongside the agricultural and industrial revolutions.
    • There is a new kind of scientist: computational scientists join theoretical and experimental scientists in astronomy, biology, chemistry, and physics.
    • Each time the cost of computing improves by another factor of 10, opportunities multiply and economically infeasible applications become practical.

    Three classes of computing applications

    The same core hardware technologies (sections 1.4 and 1.5) are used in everything from smart home appliances to cell phones to the largest supercomputers, but the design requirements differ.

Emphasizes good performance to single users at low cost and usually runs third-party software. Drove the evolution of many computing technologies, and is only about 40 years old.     Processor core: many embedded processors are designed using processor cores, a version of a processor written in a hardware description language such as Verilog or VHDL (Chapter 4). A core lets a designer integrate other application-specific hardware with the processor core on a single chip.

    The PostPC era

    • Since the book’s fourth edition, a generational change as significant as the switch to personal computers 40 years ago has happened.
    • Personal mobile devices (PMDs): battery operated, wireless Internet connectivity, typically cost hundreds of dollars, and users download software (“apps”). Unlike PCs they have no keyboard and mouse and rely on a touch-sensitive screen or speech input. Today a PMD is a smart phone or tablet, and tomorrow it may include electronic glasses.
    • Figure 1.1.5 (COD Figure 1.2) plots units sold per year in millions (axis 0–1600) from 2007 to 2018 for four lines: smart phone, cell phone (excluding smart phones), PC (excluding tablets), and tablet. Smart phones climb to roughly 1,500 million a year near the end, traditional cell phones peak around 1,300 million, PCs stay under about 400 million, and tablets under about 250 million. Smart phones passed PCs in 2011. PCs, tablets, and traditional cell phones are declining. Peak volume years: 2011 cell phones, 2013 PCs, 2014 tablets. PCs fell from 20% of units shipped in 2007 to 10% in 2018.
    • Cloud computing takes over from the traditional server. It relies on giant datacenters called Warehouse Scale Computers (WSCs). Companies like Amazon and Google build WSCs of 50,000 servers and rent portions to other companies so they can provide services to PMDs without building their own WSCs.
    • Glossary, cloud computing: large collections of servers that provide services over the Internet; some providers rent dynamically varying numbers of servers as a utility.
    • Software as a Service (SaaS): delivers software and data as a service over the Internet, usually via a thin program such as a browser on local client devices, instead of binary code installed and run wholly on the device. Examples: web search and social networking. SaaS is revolutionizing software as PMDs and WSCs revolutionize hardware.
    • Developers today often run part of an application on the PMD and part in the Cloud.

    Activity 1.1.4: “PostPC” means today’s most widely used computers aren’t PCs (true). PC sales are not increasing dramatically (false). A smart phone is a computer (so “isn’t” is false). Cloud computing often involves thousands of servers in giant warehouses (true). SaaS is not installing big applications like Microsoft Office on a PC (false).

    What you can learn from the book

    • In the 1960s and 1970s, memory size was a primary constraint, so the credo was “minimize memory space to make programs fast.” In the last two decades, better design and memory technology made small memory size unimportant except in embedded systems.
    • Performance programmers now must understand the parallel nature of processors and the hierarchical nature of memories. They also worry about energy efficiency on the PMD or in the Cloud (section 1.7, the power wall).
    • The questions the book promises to answer: how HLL programs (C, Java) are translated and executed; the hardware/software interface; what determines program performance and how to improve it; how hardware designers improve performance; how designers and programmers affect energy efficiency; why the switch from sequential to parallel processing happened (Chapter 6); and what great ideas architects have had since the first commercial computer in 1951.
    • More on hardware performance techniques is in the authors’ advanced book, Computer Architecture: A Quantitative Approach.
    • Without these answers, improving performance is trial and error rather than a scientific procedure driven by insight and analysis.
    • Chapter 1 introduces basic definitions, puts hardware and software in perspective, shows how to evaluate performance and energy, introduces integrated circuits, and explains the shift to multicores.
    Multicore microprocessor: a microprocessor containing multiple processors (“cores”) in a single integrated circuit.
    Acronym: a word constructed by taking the initial letters of a string of words. Examples: RAM (Random Access Memory) and CPU (Central Processing Unit). The book says you’ll soon use BIOS, CPU, DIMM, DRAM, PCIe, SATA correctly. Key terms get a definition in a colored box near their first appearance.

    1.2 Seven great ideas in computer architecture

    Architects invented these ideas over the last 60 years of computer design. They outlasted the first computers that used them, and newer architects show their admiration by imitating their predecessors. The book uses icons and highlighted terms to mark the nearly 100 sections that use them.

    Great ideaIconWhat the book says
    Use abstraction to simplify designAbstract paintingArchitects and programmers had to invent productivity techniques, or design time would grow as dramatically as resources grew. Abstractions represent the design at different levels. Lower-level details are hidden to offer a simpler model at higher levels.
    Make the common case fastSports carEnhances performance more than optimizing the rare case. Ironically, the common case is often simpler, so it’s easier to enhance. You need to know what the common case is, which takes careful experimentation and measurement (section 1.6). The most common trip has one or two passengers, and a fast sports car is easier to build than a fast minivan.
    Performance via parallelismMultiple jet engines of a planeSince the dawn of computing, architects have gotten more performance by doing operations in parallel.
    Performance via pipeliningA sequence of pipes, each section one stageA pattern of parallelism so common it has its own name. Multiple operations move through hardware units that each do a piece, like water through a pipeline. The bucket brigade: before fire engines, townsfolk (as in cowboy movies, responding to a villain’s dastardly act) formed a human chain to move buckets faster than individuals running back and forth.
    Performance via predictionFortune-teller’s crystal ball“Better to ask for forgiveness than permission.” Guessing and starting work can be faster on average than waiting to know for sure, if recovering from a misprediction isn’t too expensive and predictions are relatively accurate.
    Hierarchy of memoriesLayered triangleProgrammers want memory that is fast, large, and cheap: speed shapes performance, capacity limits problem size, and memory is often the majority of computer cost. The hierarchy puts the fastest, smallest, most expensive per bit at the top and the slowest, largest, cheapest per bit at the bottom. Caches (Chapter 5) give the illusion that main memory is nearly as fast as the top and nearly as big and cheap as the bottom. In the icon, closer to the top means faster and more expensive per bit, and a wider layer means bigger memory.
    Dependability via redundancyTractor-trailer truckAny physical device can fail, so systems include redundant components that take over on a failure and help detect failures. The truck’s dual tires on each side of its rear axles let it keep driving with one flat, and the driver presumably heads to a repair facility to restore redundancy.

    The dropped eighth idea: Designing for Moore’s Law

    • It was in the prior edition. Gordon Moore, one of Intel’s founders, predicted in 1965 that integrated circuit resources would double every year. A decade later he amended it to every two years.
    • It was accurate, and for 50 years it shaped computer architecture. Designs take years, so chip resources (transistors) could double or triple between the start and end of a project. Like a skeet shooter, architects aimed where the technology would be when the design finished.
    • “No exponential growth can last forever.” Moore’s Law is no longer accurate, which is shocking for designers who relied on it. Some don’t want to believe it’s over, partly because they confuse “the biannual doubling rate is now incorrect” with “semiconductors will no longer improve.” Semiconductors will keep improving, just more slowly.
    • Starting with this edition, the book discusses the implications of slowing Moore’s Law, especially in Chapter 6.
    Elaboration: in Moore’s Law’s heyday, the cost per chip resource dropped each generation. In the latest technologies, it may be flat or rising, because of the cost of new equipment, the elaborate processes needed to make chips work at finer feature sizes, and fewer companies investing to push the state of the art. Less competition naturally leads to higher prices.

    1.3 Below your program

    Opening quote: Mark Twain, The Innocents Abroad, 1869: in Paris they stared when he spoke French, and he never succeeded in making them understand their own language.

    A typical application, such as a word processor or a large database system, may have millions of lines of code and rely on sophisticated libraries. Hardware can only execute extremely simple low-level instructions. Getting from one to the other takes several layers of software that interpret or translate, which is an example of abstraction.

    Figure 1.3.1 (COD Figure 1.3) draws the layers as concentric circles: hardware in the center, systems software around it, and applications software outermost. Hardware is the physical machine that runs software. In complex applications there may be multiple application layers, for example a database system running on systems software with another application on top of the database.

    Systems software: software that provides services that are commonly useful, including operating systems, compilers, loaders, and assemblers.
    Operating system: supervising program that manages the resources of a computer for the benefit of the programs that run on that computer.
    Compiler: a program that translates high-level language statements into assembly language statements.

    Two types of systems software are central to every computer: the operating system and the compiler. The OS interfaces between a user’s program and the hardware and provides services and supervisory functions. The most important are:

    • Handling basic input and output operations
    • Allocating storage and memory
    • Providing for protected sharing of the computer among multiple applications using it simultaneously

    OS examples: Linux, iOS, Android, Windows. Compilers translate a program in a high-level language (C, C++, Java, Visual Basic) into instructions the hardware can execute. The translation is complex because languages are sophisticated and hardware instructions are simple. More detail is in Chapter 2 and Appendix A (Assemblers, Linkers, and the SPIM Simulator).

    Activity 1.3.2: a car’s steering wheel and pedals hiding complex details are abstraction. The physical machinery software runs on is hardware. Software that manages hardware resources for other programs is the operating system. A tic-tac-toe program is application software. The collection of software providing services to applications is systems software.

    From a high-level language to the language of hardware

    • The easiest signals for computers are on and off, so the computer alphabet has two letters. Just as 26 English letters don’t limit what can be written, two letters don’t limit what computers can do.
    • The symbols are 0 and 1, so computer language is numbers in base 2 (binary). Each letter is a binary digit or bit.
    • Computers are “slaves to our commands,” which are called instructions. Instructions are just collections of bits and can be thought of as numbers. For example, 1000110010100000 tells one computer to add two numbers. Using numbers for both instructions and data is a foundation of computing (Chapter 2).
    • The first programmers used binary directly. It was so tedious that they invented notations closer to how humans think, first translated to binary by hand. Then, using the computer to help program the computer, they invented programs to do the translation. The first was the assembler: add A,B becomes 1000110010100000.
    • The symbolic language is assembly language. The binary language the machine understands is machine language.
    Binary digit: also called a bit. One of the two numbers in base 2 (0 or 1) that are the components of information.
    Instruction: a command that computer hardware understands and obeys.
    Assembler: a program that translates a symbolic version of instructions into the binary version.
    Assembly language: a symbolic representation of machine instructions.
    Machine language: a binary representation of machine instructions.

    Activity 1.3.3: “bit” is short for binary digit (true). Computers use binary because on/off signals are easiest, not because binary is more powerful (false). Two letters can represent as much information as 26 (true). If memory holds 00001100 it contains 12 (true). 1000110010100000 could be a machine language instruction (true) but not an assembly instruction (false). An assembler translates add A,B into that binary (true).

    High-level languages

    Assembly is still far from what a scientist simulating fluid flow or an accountant balancing the books wants. It needs one line per machine instruction and forces you to think like the computer. Realizing that a program could translate a more powerful language into instructions was one of the great breakthroughs of early computing. Programmers owe their productivity “and their sanity” to high-level languages and compilers.

    High-level programming language: a portable language such as C, C++, Java, or Visual Basic that is composed of words and algebraic notation that can be translated by a compiler into assembly language.

    Figure 1.3.4 (COD Figure 1.4) compiles C swap(int v[], int k) into assembly for MIPS (multi, add, lw, lw, sw, sw, jr $31, seven instructions), then the assembler produces binary machine language for MIPS. Some compilers cut out the middleman and produce binary directly. The book’s smaller example: the compiler turns A + B into add A, B, and the assembler turns that into binary.

    Three benefits of high-level languages:

    1. You think in a more natural language, using English words and algebraic notation, so programs look like text rather than tables of cryptic symbols. Languages can be designed for their use: Fortran for scientific computation, Cobol for business data processing, Lisp for symbol manipulation, plus domain-specific languages for narrower groups such as machine learning.
    2. Programmer productivity. One of the few areas of widespread agreement in software development is that programs take less time to write in languages needing fewer lines. Conciseness is a clear advantage over assembly.
    3. Programs are independent of the computer they were developed on, because compilers and assemblers can translate to the binary of any computer.

    These advantages are so strong that little programming is done in assembly today.

    Activity 1.3.5: temp = v[k]; is the high-level instruction. C is a high-level language. HLLs let you think more naturally, implement a program in less time, and write programs independent of a particular machine.

    1.4 Under the covers

    All computer hardware performs the same basic functions: inputting, outputting, processing, and storing data. How these are done is the primary topic of the book. When a point is so important the authors hope you remember it forever, they label it a Big Picture. There are about a dozen, and the first is the five components.

    Input device: a mechanism through which the computer is fed information, such as a keyboard. (Text example: a microphone.)
    Output device: a mechanism that conveys the result of a computation to a user, such as a display, or to another computer. (Text example: a speaker.)
    Some devices, such as wireless networks, provide both input and output. I/O is covered in more detail in Chapters 5 and 6.
    Big Picture: the five classic components of a computer are input, output, memory, datapath, and control, with the last two sometimes combined and called the processor. This organization is independent of hardware technology: every piece of every computer, past and present, fits one of these five categories.

    Figure 1.4.1 (COD Figure 1.5): the processor gets instructions and data from memory. Input writes data to memory. Output reads data from memory. Control sends the signals that determine the operations of the datapath, memory, input, and output. Animation: input from a keyboard, file, network, and so on is written to memory, and a datapath operates on data. Activity 1.4.2 matches: input writes to memory (e.g., keyboard), output reads from memory (e.g., display), memory stores programs and data, control sends signals that determine other components’ operation, and the datapath performs computations.

    Through the looking glass (displays)

    • The most fascinating I/O device is probably the graphics display. Most PMDs use liquid crystal displays (LCDs) for a thin, low-power display.
    • The LCD is not the source of light. It controls the transmission of light. Rod-shaped molecules in a liquid form a twisting helix that bends light entering from a light source behind the display or, less often, from reflected light. When a current is applied the rods straighten out and no longer bend light. The liquid crystal is between two screens polarized at 90 degrees, so light can’t pass unless it’s bent.
    • Most LCDs today use an active matrix with a tiny transistor switch at each pixel to precisely control current and make sharper images. A red-green-blue mask at each dot sets the intensity of the three color components. A color active matrix LCD has three transistor switches at each point.
    Liquid crystal display: a display technology using a thin layer of liquid polymers that can be used to transmit or block light according to whether a charge is applied.
    Active matrix display: a liquid crystal display using a transistor to control the transmission of light at each individual pixel.
    Pixel: the smallest individual picture element. Screens are composed of hundreds of thousands to millions of pixels, organized in a matrix.
    • The image is a matrix of pixels represented as a matrix of bits called a bit map. A typical tablet’s display matrix ranges from 1024 × 768 to 2048 × 1536. A color display might use 8 bits for each of red, blue, and green, for 24 bits per pixel, permitting millions of colors.
    • Hardware support for graphics is mainly a raster refresh buffer, or frame buffer, that stores the bit map. The bit pattern per pixel is read out to the display at the refresh rate.
    • Figure 1.4.3 (COD Figure 1.6) shows a frame buffer with a simplified 4 bits per pixel feeding a raster scan CRT display. Each pixel has an (X, Y) coordinate. In the example, pixel (X0, Y0) has pattern 0011 and pixel (X1, Y1) has 1011, and the screen reads “Basketball starts soon!”
    • The goal of the bit map is to faithfully represent the screen. Graphics is hard because the human eye is very good at detecting even subtle changes.
    • Ivan Sutherland, the “father” of computer graphics, Scientific American, 1984: through computer displays he landed an airplane on the deck of a moving carrier, observed a nuclear particle hit a potential well, flew in a rocket at nearly the speed of light, and watched a computer reveal its innermost workings.

    Touchscreens

    PCs also use LCDs, but PostPC tablets and smartphones replaced the keyboard and mouse with touch-sensitive displays, so users point directly at what they want instead of indirectly with a mouse. There are many ways to build one, but many tablets use capacitive sensing: people are electrical conductors, so if an insulator like glass is covered with a transparent conductor, touching distorts the screen’s electrostatic field, changing the capacitance. It allows multiple touches simultaneously, enabling gestures.

    Activity 1.4.4: an LCD does not generate colored light (false). A display has hundreds of thousands to millions of pixels, not hundreds (false). Active matrix uses a transistor per pixel (true). 24 bits per pixel, 8 each for RGB (true). A frame buffer stores pixel values (true). A touchscreen combines a display for output with a touch-sensitive screen for input (true).

    Integrated circuit: also called a chip. A device combining dozens to millions of transistors.
    Central processor unit (CPU): also called processor. The active part of the computer, which contains the datapath and control and which adds numbers, tests numbers, signals I/O devices to activate, and so on.
    Datapath: the component of the processor that performs arithmetic operations.
    Control: the component of the processor that commands the datapath, memory, and I/O devices according to the instructions of the program.
    Memory: the storage area in which programs are kept when they are running and that contains the data needed by the running programs.
    DRAM: memory built as an integrated circuit; it provides random access to any location. Access times are 50 nanoseconds and cost per gigabyte in 2020 was $3 to $6.

    Datapath and control are the brawn and brain of the processor. Chapter 4 explains them for a higher-performance design. DRAMs together contain a program’s instructions and data. In contrast to sequential access memories such as magnetic tapes, the RAM in DRAM means accesses take basically the same time no matter what portion is read.

(The activity also asks how many chips the logic board has, with choices 1, 2, or 7; count them in Figure 1.8 in your book, since the text doesn’t state it.) The A12 has 2 large ARM processors. A CPU is also called a processor.

    Cache

    Inside the processor is another memory: cache, a small, fast memory acting as a buffer for DRAM. The nontechnical definition of cache is “a safe place for hiding things.” Cache is built from SRAM, which is faster but less dense, and hence more expensive, than DRAM. SRAM and DRAM are two layers of the memory hierarchy.

    Cache memory: a small, fast memory that acts as a buffer for a slower, larger memory.
    Static random access memory (SRAM): also memory built as an integrated circuit, but faster and less dense than DRAM.

    Activity 1.4.6: DRAM is the large memory where most data is stored. SRAM is faster than DRAM but uses more area per bit. Cache keeps a copy of data from a larger memory.

    The ISA abstraction

    One of the most important abstractions is the interface between hardware and the lowest-level software. Software talks to hardware through a vocabulary. The words are instructions, and the vocabulary is the instruction set architecture, or simply architecture. It includes anything programmers need to make a binary machine language program work correctly, including instructions and I/O devices. The OS typically encapsulates I/O, memory allocation, and other low-level functions so application programmers don’t need to worry about them.

    Instruction set architecture: also called architecture. An abstract interface between the hardware and the lowest-level software that encompasses all the information necessary to write a machine language program that will run correctly, including instructions, registers, memory access, I/O, and so on.
    Application binary interface (ABI): the user portion of the instruction set plus the operating system interfaces used by application programmers. It defines a standard for binary portability across computers.
    Implementation: hardware that obeys the architecture abstraction.

    An ISA lets designers talk about functions independently of hardware, like a digital clock: keeping time, displaying time, and setting the alarm are functions, and the quartz crystal, LED displays, and plastic buttons are hardware.

    Big Picture: both hardware and software consist of hierarchical layers using abstraction, each lower layer hiding details from the level above. One key interface is the ISA, between hardware and low-level software. It enables many implementations of varying cost and performance to run identical software.

    Activity 1.4.7: an ISA lets a machine language program run on different implementations (true). Designers do not try to keep new implementations’ performance the same as old ones (false).

    A safe place for data

    • If power is lost, everything in the computer’s memory is lost, because it is volatile. A DVD disk doesn’t forget the movie when the player is off, so it is nonvolatile.
    • Main memory (primary memory) holds data and programs while running. Secondary memory stores them between runs and forms the next lower layer of the memory hierarchy.
    • DRAMs have dominated main memory since 1975, but magnetic disks dominated secondary memory even earlier.
    • Because of size and form factor, PMDs use flash memory instead of disks. Compared with DRAM, flash is slower but much cheaper and nonvolatile. Compared with disks, it costs more per bit, but it is smaller, comes in much smaller capacities, is more rugged, and is more power efficient. So flash is the standard secondary memory for PMDs.
    • Flash bits wear out after 100,000 to 1,000,000 writes, unlike disks and DRAM. File systems must track writes and avoid wearing out storage, for example by moving popular data. Chapter 5 covers disks and flash in more detail.
    Volatile memory: storage, such as DRAM, that retains data only if it is receiving power.
    Nonvolatile memory: a form of memory that retains data even in the absence of a power source and that is used to store programs between runs. A DVD disk is nonvolatile.
    Main memory: also called primary memory. Memory used to hold programs while they are running; typically consists of DRAM in today’s computers.
    Secondary memory: nonvolatile memory used to store programs and data between runs; typically consists of flash memory in PMDs and magnetic disks in servers.
    Magnetic disk: also called hard disk. A form of nonvolatile secondary memory composed of rotating platters coated with a magnetic recording material. Because they are rotating mechanical devices, access times are about 5 to 20 milliseconds and cost per gigabyte in 2020 was $0.01 to $0.02.
    Flash memory: a nonvolatile semiconductor memory. It is cheaper and slower than DRAM but more expensive per bit and faster than magnetic disks. Access times are about 5 to 50 microseconds and cost per gigabyte in 2020 was $0.06 to $0.12.

    Communicating with other computers

    Networks interconnect whole computers. They are the backbone of current computer systems: a new PMD or server without a network interface would be ridiculed. Advantages:

    • Communication: information is exchanged between computers at high speeds.
    • Resource sharing: computers on the network can share I/O devices rather than each having its own.
    • Nonlocal access: by connecting over long distances, users need not be near the computer they use.
    • Cost of communication increases with both the speed and the distance information travels.
    • Ethernet is perhaps the most popular network: up to a kilometer long and up to 100 gigabits per second. That makes it useful on the same floor of a building, so it is a local area network. LANs are interconnected with switches that can also provide routing services and security.
    • Wide area networks cross continents and are the backbone of the Internet, which supports the web. They are typically based on optical fibers and leased from telecommunication companies.
    • Networks changed computing over the last 40 years. In the 1970s, very few people had email, the Internet and web didn’t exist, and physically mailing magnetic tapes was the primary way to move large data. LANs were almost nonexistent, and the few WANs had limited capacity and restricted access.
    • The first standardized LAN, developed about 40 years ago, was a version of Ethernet with a maximum capacity (bandwidth) of 10 million bits per second, typically shared by tens or even a hundred computers. Today LANs offer 1 to 100 gigabits per second, usually shared by at most a few computers.
    • Optical communications grew WAN capacity from hundreds of kilobits to gigabits, and from hundreds of computers to millions connected worldwide. Deployment plus capacity made networks central to the information revolution of the last 30 years.
    • For the last 15 years, wireless has reshaped communication and enabled the PostPC era. Building a radio in the same low-cost semiconductor technology (CMOS) used for memory and microprocessors dropped the price and caused an explosion in deployment. The IEEE standard 802.11ac allows 1 to 1300 million bits per second. Unlike wired networks, all users in an area share the airwaves.
    Local area network (LAN): a network designed to carry data within a geographically confined area, typically within a single building.
    Wide area network (WAN): a network extended over hundreds of kilometers that can span a continent.

    Silicon

    Manufacture begins with silicon, a substance found in sand. It doesn’t conduct electricity well, so it is a semiconductor. A special chemical process adds materials to silicon so tiny areas become one of three devices:

    • Excellent conductors of electricity (microscopic copper or aluminum wire)
    • Excellent insulators (like plastic sheathing or glass)
    • Areas that conduct or insulate under special conditions (a switch). Transistors fall in this last category.
    Silicon: a natural element that is a semiconductor.
    Semiconductor: a substance that does not conduct electricity well.
    Silicon crystal ingot: a rod composed of a silicon crystal that is between 8 and 12 inches in diameter and about 12 to 24 inches long.
    Wafer: a slice from a silicon ingot no more than 0.1 inches thick, used to create chips.
    Defect: a microscopic flaw in a wafer or in patterning steps that can result in the failure of the die containing that defect.
    Die: the individual rectangular sections that are cut from a wafer, more informally known as chips.
    Yield: the percentage of good dies from the total number of dies on the wafer.

    The manufacturing process (COD Figure 1.12)

    The manufacturing process is critical to chip cost, so it matters to designers. The ingot “looks like a giant sausage.”

    1. Silicon ingot → slicer → blank wafers.
    2. 20 to 40 processing steps place patterns of chemicals on each wafer, creating transistors, conductors, and insulators → patterned wafers. ICs have one layer of transistors but two to eight levels of metal conductor, separated by insulator layers.
    3. Wafer tester makes a map of the good parts → tested wafer.
    4. Dicer cuts the wafer into dies → tested dies.
    5. Good dies are bonded to packages: connected to the input/output pins of a package, a process called bonding.
    6. Part tester tests packaged dies a final time, since mistakes can occur in packaging. In the animation, one bad packaged part is found in this final test.
    7. Ship to customers.

    A single microscopic flaw in the wafer or in one of the dozens of patterning steps can make that area fail. These defects make a perfect wafer virtually impossible. The simplest way to cope is to place many independent components on a single wafer and dice it, so you discard only the unlucky dies rather than the whole wafer.

    Cost and yield

    • IC cost rises quickly with die size, due to both lower yield and fewer dies per wafer.
    • The next-generation process shrinks a large die by using smaller transistors and wires, improving both yield and die count per wafer.
    • A 7-nanometer process was state of the art in 2020: the smallest feature size on the die is essentially 7 nm.

    Cost vs. price

    Companies charge as much as the market will bear to maximize return on investment, which must cover R&D, marketing, sales, manufacturing equipment maintenance, building rental, cost of financing, pretax profits, and taxes. Margins can be higher on unique chips from one company (microprocessors) than on commodity chips from several companies (DRAMs). Price fluctuates with the ratio of supply and demand, and it is easy for multiple companies to build more chips than the market demands.

    Elaboration: the three cost equations.
    Cost per die = Cost per wafer ÷ (Dies per wafer × Yield)
    Dies per wafer ≈ Wafer area ÷ Die area
    Yield = 1 ÷ (1 + (Defects per area × Die area ÷ 2))2
    The first is straightforward to derive. The second is an approximation, because it doesn’t subtract the area near the border of the round wafer that can’t hold rectangular dies. The third is based on empirical observations of yields at IC factories, with the exponent related to the number of critical processing steps. So depending on defect rate and die and wafer size, costs are generally not linear in die area.

Masks are expensive, so cost per chip drops (true). Engineering costs are high and largely volume-independent (true). High-volume parts always have smaller dies (false).

    Measuring performance

    • Time is the measure of computer performance. Execution time is measured in seconds per program.
    • Wall clock time, response time, or elapsed time: total time including disk accesses, memory accesses, I/O, OS overhead, “everything.”
    • Computers are often shared, so the system may optimize throughput rather than one program’s elapsed time. CPU execution time (CPU time) is the time the CPU spends computing for this task, not including I/O waits or other programs. The user still experiences elapsed time, not CPU time.
    • CPU time = user CPU time (in the program) + system CPU time (in the OS on the program’s behalf). They are hard to separate accurately, because it’s hard to assign responsibility for OS activities to one program, and because OSs differ in functionality.
    • The book uses system performance for elapsed time on an unloaded system, and CPU performance for user CPU time. Chapter 1 focuses on CPU performance.
    CPU execution time: also called CPU time. The actual time the CPU spends computing for a specific task.
    User CPU time: the CPU time spent in a program itself.
    System CPU time: the CPU time spent in the operating system performing tasks on behalf of the program.

    Activity 1.6.5: the task runs 5 ms, the OS does disk work 4 ms, the CPU idles 2 ms, and the task runs 10 ms. Elapsed = 21, user CPU = 15, CPU time = 19, system performance = 21, CPU performance = 15 (all ms).

    Understanding program performance: different applications are sensitive to different aspects. Many applications, especially on servers, depend as much on I/O performance, which relies on hardware and software. Wall-clock elapsed time is the measurement of interest. A user might care about throughput, response time, or a combination (e.g., maximum throughput with a worst-case response time). To improve performance, first define clearly which metric matters, then look for bottlenecks by measuring execution.

    Clocks

    • Designers think in terms of how fast hardware performs basic functions. Almost all computers use a clock that determines when events happen.
    • The discrete intervals are clock cycles (also ticks, clock ticks, clock periods, clocks, cycles). Designers describe them by the clock period (e.g., 250 ps) or the clock rate (e.g., 4 GHz). Clock rate is the inverse of clock period.
    Clock cycle: also called tick, clock tick, clock period, clock, or cycle. The time for one clock period, usually of the processor clock, which runs at a constant rate.
    Clock period: the length of each clock cycle.

    Activity 1.6.6: 1 GHz ticks one billion times per second (true). 1 GHz is a 1 ns period, 10−9 s (true).
    Check yourself 1.6.7 (network-limited app): an extra channel that raises throughput and cuts access delay improves both. Better networking software that cuts delay but not throughput improves only response time. Adding memory improves neither.

    CPU performance and its factors

    CPU execution time for a program = CPU clock cycles for a program × Clock cycle time
    CPU execution time for a program = CPU clock cycles for a program / Clock rate

    A designer can improve performance by reducing the number of clock cycles or the length of the clock cycle. There is often a trade-off: many techniques that reduce cycle count also increase cycle time.

    Example 1.6.2: the program takes 10 s on A with a 2 GHz clock. B should take 6 s. A big clock rate increase is possible but makes B need 1.2× as many cycles.

    CyclesA = 10 s × 2×109 cycles/s = 20×109 cycles
    6 s = 1.2 × 20×109 / Clock rateB → Clock rateB = 24×109 / 6 = 4×109 = 4 GHz, twice A’s clock rate

    Activity 1.6.9: A needs 20×109 cycles. B improves performance by reducing the length of a clock cycle.

    Instruction performance

    CPU clock cycles = Instructions for a program × Average clock cycles per instruction

    CPI is the average number of clock cycles each instruction takes. Different instructions take different times, so CPI is an average over all instructions executed. It lets you compare two implementations of the same ISA, because instruction count is the same.

    Clock cycles per instruction (CPI): average number of clock cycles per instruction for a program or program fragment.

    Example 1.6.3: same ISA. A: 250 ps cycle, CPI 2.0. B: 500 ps cycle, CPI 1.2. With I instructions:

    CyclesA = 2.0 × I, CyclesB = 1.2 × I
    CPU timeA = 2.0 × I × 250 ps = 500 × I ps
    CPU timeB = 1.2 × I × 500 ps = 600 × I ps
    600 × I / 500 × I = 1.2: A is 1.2 times as fast

    Activity 1.6.10: they execute the same number of instructions because they use the same ISA. A has the faster clock. A needs more cycles per instruction (B needs fewer). For 1000 instructions, A’s time is 1000 × 2.0 × 250 ps = 500,000 ps = 500 ns. B executes the same 1000 instructions. For 2000 instructions, A is 1.2 times faster. Is A “better” than B? Unclear: this is only one program.

    The classic CPU performance equation

    CPU time = Instruction count × CPI × Clock cycle time
    CPU time = (Instruction count × CPI) / Clock rate

    These separate the three key factors that affect performance, so you can compare implementations or evaluate a design alternative if you know its effect on the three.

    Instruction count: the number of instructions executed by the program.

    Example 1.6.4 (comparing code segments): CPI is 1 for class A, 2 for B, 3 for C. Sequence 1 counts are (2, 1, 2). Sequence 2 counts are (4, 1, 1).

    Sequence 1: 2 + 1 + 2 = 5 instructions; cycles = 2×1 + 1×2 + 2×3 = 10; CPI = 10/5 = 2.0
    Sequence 2: 4 + 1 + 1 = 6 instructions; cycles = 4×1 + 1×2 + 1×3 = 9; CPI = 9/6 = 1.5
    Sequence 2 is faster even though it executes one extra instruction.

    Big Picture: execution time in seconds per program = (Instructions / Program) × (Clock cycles / Instruction) × (Seconds / Clock cycle). The only complete and reliable measure of computer performance is time. Changing the ISA to lower instruction count can lead to a slower clock cycle or higher CPI that offsets it. And because CPI depends on the instruction mix, the code with the fewest instructions may not be the fastest.

    How to get the numbers

    • CPU execution time: run the program. Clock cycle time: usually published in the computer’s documentation. Instruction count and CPI are harder. If you know clock rate and CPU time, you need only one of instruction count or CPI to find the other.
    • Instruction count: software tools that profile execution, a simulator of the architecture, or hardware counters (included in most processors), which can record instructions executed, average CPI, and often the sources of performance loss.
    • Instruction count depends on the architecture but not the exact implementation, so you can measure it without knowing implementation details.
    • CPI depends on many design details, including the memory system and processor structure (Chapters 4 and 5), and on the mix of instruction types. So CPI varies by application and among implementations of the same ISA.

    Instruction mix: a measure of the dynamic frequency of instructions across one or many programs.

    1.11 Fallacies and pitfalls

    Opening quote: Sir Karl Popper, The Philosophy of Science, 1957: “Science must begin with myths, and the criticism of myths.”

    Every chapter has a fallacies and pitfalls section. Fallacies are commonly held misconceptions, and the book tries to give a counterexample for each. Pitfalls are easily made mistakes, often generalizations of principles that are true only in a limited context. Cost/performance fallacies and pitfalls “have ensnared many a computer architect, including us,” so there is no shortage of examples.

    Pitfall: expecting the improvement of one aspect of a computer to increase overall performance by an amount proportional to the size of the improvement

    This is the demoralizing corollary of “make the common case fast” that has plagued designers of both hardware and software: the opportunity for improvement is affected by how much time the event consumes.

    Execution time after improvement = (Execution time affected by improvement / Amount of improvement) + Execution time unaffected

    Animation 1.11.1: a program runs 100 s; adds take 60 s, everything else 40 s.

    • Adds 2× faster: 60/2 + 40 = 70 s, speedup 1.4.
    • Adds 4× faster: 60/4 + 40 = 55 s, speedup 1.8.
    • Adds in 0 s: still 40 s, speedup 2.5.

    Design problem (1.11.2): a program runs 100 s, with multiply taking 80 s. How much faster must multiply get for the program to run five times faster?

    100/5 = 20 s = 80 s / n + (100 − 80) s → 20 = 80/n + 20 → 0 = 80/n

    No amount of multiply improvement gives a fivefold increase if multiply is only 80% of the workload. In everyday life, this is the law of diminishing returns.

    Amdahl’s Law: a rule stating that the performance enhancement possible with a given improvement is limited by the amount that the improved feature is used. It is a quantitative version of the law of diminishing returns.
    • Use Amdahl’s Law to estimate improvements when you know the time a function consumes and its potential speedup. With the CPU performance equation, it is a handy tool for evaluating enhancements. More in the exercises.
    • Amdahl’s Law is also used to argue for practical limits on the number of parallel processors (Chapter 6’s fallacies and pitfalls).

    Activity 1.11.3: 100 s program, multiply 30 s, made 2× faster: 15 + 70 = 85 s. If an aspect is 50% of time, the limit is 2 times faster.

    Fallacy: computers at low utilization use little power

    • Power efficiency matters at low utilization because server workloads vary.
    • Servers in Google’s warehouse scale computer run at 10% to 50% utilization most of the time and at 100% less than 1% of the time.
    • Even after five years to learn to run the SPECpower benchmark well, the specially configured computer with the best results in 2020 still uses 33% of peak power at 10% load. Systems in the field not configured for SPECpower are surely worse.
    • Luiz Barroso and Urs Hölzle [2007] argue for redesigning hardware for “energy-proportional computing.” If servers used, say, 10% of peak power at 10% workload, datacenters would cut electricity bills and be good corporate citizens in an era of concern about CO2 emissions.

    Fallacy: designing for performance and designing for energy efficiency are unrelated goals

    Energy is power over time, so optimizations that take less time often save energy overall even if they use a bit more energy when used. The rest of the computer consumes energy the whole time the program runs, so reducing time saves the whole system’s energy.

    Activity 1.11.4: Google’s WSC does not use 5% of peak power at 10% utilization (false; the best SPECpower system uses 33%). Designing for performance does not necessarily increase energy (false).

    Pitfall: using a subset of the performance equation as a performance metric

    Predicting performance from just one of clock rate, instruction count, or CPI is dangerous. Another common mistake is using only two of the three. That may be valid in a limited context, but it’s easily misused. Nearly all proposed alternatives to time have led to misleading claims, distorted results, or incorrect interpretations.

    MIPS = Instruction count / (Execution time × 106)
    MIPS = Instruction count / ((Instruction count × CPI / Clock rate) × 106) = Clock rate / (CPI × 106)
    Million instructions per second (MIPS): a measurement of program execution speed based on the number of millions of instructions. MIPS is computed as the instruction count divided by the product of the execution time and 106.

    MIPS is an instruction execution rate, so it specifies performance inversely to execution time. The good news: it’s easy to understand, and faster computers mean bigger MIPS, which matches intuition. The three problems:

    1. It specifies the execution rate but not the capabilities of the instructions, so you can’t compare computers with different instruction sets.
    2. It varies between programs on the same computer, so a computer can’t have a single MIPS rating. CPI varied by a factor of 4 for SPECspeed 2017 Integer on a 1.8 GHz Intel Xeon E5-2650L (COD Figure 1.18), so MIPS does too.
    3. Most importantly, if a new program executes more instructions but each is faster, MIPS can vary independently from performance.

    Check yourself 1.11.5: A has 10 billion instructions, 4 GHz, CPI 1.0. B has 8 billion, 4 GHz, CPI 1.1. A’s MIPS = 4000, B’s ≈ 3636, so A has the higher MIPS. A’s time = 10×109 × 1.0 / 4×109 = 2.5 s. B’s = 8×109 × 1.1 / 4×109 = 2.2 s. B is faster.

    2.2 Operations of the computer hardware

    Opening quote: Burks, Goldstine, and von Neumann, 1947: “There must certainly be instructions for performing the fundamental arithmetic operations.”

    • Every computer must be able to do arithmetic. ADD a, b, c adds b and c and puts the sum in a. If a is 3 and b is 4, ADD a, a, b overwrites a with 7.
    • The notation is rigid: each LEGv8 arithmetic instruction performs only one operation and must always have exactly three variables. The book is deliberately vague here about what a “variable” is (explained in 2.3).
    • Summing b, c, d, and e into a takes three instructions: ADD a,b,c, ADD a,a,d, ADD a,a,e.
    • Words after // are comments the computer ignores. Unlike other languages, each line can contain at most one instruction. Unlike C, comments always terminate at the end of a line.
    • SUB a, b, c computes b − c and puts it in a.

    Activity 2.2.4 (b = 2, c = 5, d = 1): ADD a,b,c gives 7. ADD t,d,c; ADD a,t,c gives t = 6, a = 11. SUB t,c,b; ADD a,t,d gives t = 3, a = 4.

    Three is the natural number of operands for addition: the two numbers being added and a place for the sum. Requiring exactly three, no more and no less, keeps the hardware simple, because hardware for a variable number of operands is more complicated.

    Design Principle 1: Simplicity favors regularity. (The first of three underlying principles of hardware design.)

    Compiling C to LEGv8

    a = b + c;          ADD a, b, c     // e.g., 2 + 6 = 8 into a

SUB f, t0, t1       // f = t0 - t1

    2.3 Operands of the computer hardware

    Unlike high-level programs, the operands of arithmetic instructions are restricted to a limited number of special hardware locations called registers. Registers are hardware design primitives that are also visible to the programmer, so you can think of them as “the bricks of computer construction.” A LEGv8 register is 64 bits; groups of 64 bits are so common they’re called a doubleword. Another popular size, 32 bits, is a word.

    Word: a natural unit of access in a computer, usually a group of 32 bits.
    Doubleword: another natural unit of access in a computer, usually a group of 64 bits; corresponds to the size of a register in the LEGv8 architecture.
    • A major difference between variables and registers is the limited number of registers, typically 32 on current computers like LEGv8. The book now adds the restriction that the three operands of arithmetic instructions must each be one of the 64-bit registers.
    Design Principle 2: Smaller is faster. A very large number of registers may increase the clock cycle time simply because electronic signals take longer when they must travel farther.
    • Guidelines like this are not absolutes: 31 registers may not be faster than 32. But the truth behind them makes designers take them seriously. The designer must balance programs’ “craving” for more registers with keeping the clock cycle fast.
    • Another reason not to use more than 32 is the number of bits it would take in the instruction format.
    • Animation 2.3.1: the clock signal needs time to reach every register. With fewer registers the clock is 1.4 GHz; with more registers it must be slower, 1.2 GHz.
    • Chapter 4 shows registers’ central role in hardware. Effective use of registers is critical to program performance.
    • You could write registers as numbers 0–31, but the LEGv8 convention is X followed by the register number, except for a few special names covered later.

    Activity 2.3.2: each operand of an arithmetic instruction comes from a register. Each LEGv8 register is 64 bits. More registers may lead to a slower clock frequency.

    Example 2.3.1: it is the compiler’s job to associate program variables with registers. With f, g, h, i, j in X19–X23, plus temporaries X9 and X10:

    Memory operands

    • Languages have simple variables and also composite data structures, arrays and structures, which can hold many more elements than there are registers. The processor keeps only a small amount of data in registers, but memory holds billions of data elements, so data structures are kept in memory.
    • Arithmetic happens only on registers, so LEGv8 needs data transfer instructions. To access a word or doubleword, the instruction supplies the memory address.
    • Memory is just a large, single-dimensional array with the address as the index, starting at 0.
    • Figure 2.3.1 (COD Figure 2.2) uses simplified word addresses: address 0 holds 1, address 1 holds 101, address 2 holds 10, address 3 holds 100. The third element has address 2, and memory[2] is 10. These addresses would be wrong for doublewords, because LEGv8 uses byte addressing.
    Data transfer instruction: a command that moves data between memory and registers.
    Address: a value used to delineate the location of a specific data element within a memory array.

    Activity 2.3.4: the word at address 3 is 100, and at address 1 is 101.

    • Copying data from memory to a register is traditionally called load. Format: operation name, the register to load, then a register and a constant used to access memory. The sum of the constant and the second register’s contents is the memory address. The real name is LDUR, for load register.
    • Elaboration: the U in LDUR stands for unscaled immediate, as opposed to scaled immediate (explained in section 2.19).
    • Base address: the starting address of an array in memory. Base register: a register that holds an array’s base address. Offset: a constant value added to a base address to locate a particular element.
    • Simplified animation 2.3.5 (before byte addressing): g = h + A[8] with g in X20, h in X21, and A a 100-doubleword array based at 5000 in X22. LDUR X9, [X22, #8] then ADD X20, X21, X9. If A[8] = 25 and h = 35, g = 60.

In COD Figure 2.3 the doublewords are at byte addresses 0, 8, 16, 24; the byte address of the third doubleword is 16. The first doubleword is bytes 0 through 7.

LEGv8 can work either as big-endian or little-endian.     Hardware/Software Interface (spilling): many programs have more variables than there are registers. The compiler keeps the most frequently used variables in registers and places the rest in memory, using loads and stores to move them. Putting less frequently used variables (or those needed later) into memory is spilling registers. By “smaller is faster,” memory must be slower than registers, since there are fewer registers, and that is true. Data are also more useful in a register: an arithmetic instruction can read two registers, operate, and write the result, while a data transfer instruction only reads or writes one operand without operating on it. So registers take less time to access, have higher throughput, are simpler to use, and use much less energy. For highest performance and energy savings, an ISA must have enough registers, and compilers must use them efficiently.
    Elaboration: for 64-bit data in 2015, registers are roughly 200 times faster (0.25 vs. 50 ns) and 10,000 times more energy efficient (0.1 vs. 1000 picojoules) than DRAM. These large differences led to caches (Chapter 5).

    Constant or immediate operands

    • Programs often use constants, for example to increment an index to the next array element. More than half of LEGv8 arithmetic instructions have a constant operand when running SPEC CPU2006.
    • Without immediates you’d load a constant from memory (placed there when the program was loaded): 
      LDUR X9, [X20, AddrConstant4]   // X9 = constant 4
ADD  X22, X22, X9               // X22 = X22 + X9 (X9 == 4)
      assuming X20 + AddrConstant4 is the memory address of the constant 4.
    • Instead, the quick add with one constant operand is add immediate (ADDI): ADDI X22, X22, #4. Constants in instructions make operations much faster and use less energy than loading them.
    • The constant zero also simplifies the instruction set: a move is just an add with a zero operand. LEGv8 dedicates XZR, hard-wired to zero, which is register number 31.
    • Using frequency to justify including constants is another example of making the common case fast.

    2.4 Signed and unsigned numbers

    • Humans think in base 10, but numbers can be in any base: 123ten = 1111011two.
    • Hardware keeps numbers as series of high and low electronic signals, so they’re base 2. Base 10 numbers are decimal, base 2 numbers are binary.
    • A binary digit is the “atom” of computing, since all information is built of bits. Its two values can be thought of as high or low, on or off, true or false, 1 or 0.
    Binary digit: also called binary bit. One of the two numbers in base 2, 0 or 1, that are the components of information.
    In any base, the value of the ith digit d is d × Basei, where i starts at 0 and increases from right to left.
    1011two = (1×23) + (0×22) + (1×21) + (1×20) = 8 + 0 + 2 + 1 = 11ten

    The book subscripts decimal numbers with ten and binary with two. The binary tool shows the weights 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1 (27 to 20). Activity 2.4.3 asks for 1110two in base ten (14).

    Bits are numbered 0, 1, 2, 3, … from right to left. The book draws a doubleword as two 32-bit rows (bits 63–32 and 31–0) holding 1011two in bits 3–0. Since doublewords are drawn vertically as well as horizontally, “leftmost” and “rightmost” may be unclear, so the book uses least significant bit for the rightmost (bit 0) and most significant bit for the leftmost (bit 63).

    Least significant bit: the rightmost bit in an LEGv8 doubleword.
    Most significant bit: the leftmost bit in an LEGv8 doubleword.

    Activity 2.4.4: the leftmost bit is numbered 63. In 1000…0000, the MSB is 1.

    A 64-bit doubleword represents 264 patterns: 0 to 264 − 1 (18,446,744,073,709,551,615ten)
    00…0000 = 0, 00…0001 = 1, 00…0010 = 2, …, 11…1101 = 18,446,744,073,709,551,613, 11…1110 = …614, 11…1111 = …615
    Value = (x63 × 263) + (x62 × 262) + … + (x1 × 21) + (x0 × 20)

    These positive numbers are called unsigned numbers.

    Activity 2.4.5: the largest 4-bit value is 15, 8-bit is 255, 32-bit is about 4 billion, and 64-bit is 264 − 1.

    Hardware/Software Interface: base 2 isn’t natural to humans, who have 10 fingers. The first commercial computer did offer decimal arithmetic, but it still used on and off signals, so a decimal digit was just several binary digits. Decimal was so inefficient that later computers reverted to all binary, converting to base 10 only for the relatively infrequent input/output events.
    • Bit patterns are just representatives of numbers. Numbers really have an infinite number of digits, almost all 0 except a few rightmost ones. We just don’t show leading 0s.
    • Hardware can add, subtract, multiply, and divide bit patterns. If the proper result can’t be represented by the rightmost hardware bits, overflow occurred. The programming language, the operating system, and the program decide what to do.

    Sign and magnitude

    The obvious way to represent signs is a separate sign bit: sign and magnitude, where one bit is the sign and the rest the magnitude. Its shortcomings:

    1. It’s not obvious where to put the sign bit. To the right? To the left? Early computers tried both.
    2. Adders may need an extra step to set the sign, because you can’t know the proper sign in advance.
    3. A separate sign bit means both a positive and a negative zero, which can cause problems for inattentive programmers.

    So sign and magnitude was soon abandoned.

    Two’s complement

    The search for an alternative asked what happens with unsigned numbers when you subtract a large number from a small one: it tries to borrow from a string of leading 0s, leaving a string of leading 1s. With no obviously better alternative, the final solution was the representation that made the hardware simple: leading 0s mean positive and leading 1s mean negative.

    0 to 9,223,372,036,854,775,807ten (263 − 1) use the same patterns as unsigned (leading 0).
    1000…0000two = −9,223,372,036,854,775,808ten (−263), the most negative number
    1000…0001two = −9,223,372,036,854,775,807, …, 1111…1101 = −3, 1111…1110 = −2, 1111…1111 = −1
    Two’s complement: a signed number representation where a leading 0 indicates a positive number and a leading 1 indicates a negative number. The complement of a value is obtained by complementing each bit (0 → 1 or 1 → 0), and then adding one to the result.
    • One negative number has no positive counterpart: −9,223,372,036,854,775,808. The imbalance worried inattentive programmers too, but sign and magnitude had problems for both programmers and hardware designers. So every computer today uses two’s complement for signed numbers.
    • All negative numbers have a 1 in the MSB, so hardware tests only this bit (0 is considered positive). This bit is the sign bit.
    Value = (x63 × −263) + (x62 × 262) + (x61 × 261) + … + (x1 × 21) + (x0 × 20)

    Hardware/Software Interface (loads): signed vs. unsigned applies to loads too. A signed load copies the sign repeatedly to fill the rest of the register, called sign extension; its purpose is a correct representation of the number in the register. Unsigned loads fill with 0s to the left. For a 64-bit doubleword into a 64-bit register the point is moot: signed and unsigned loads are identical. ARMv8 offers two byte loads: load byte (LDURB) treats the byte as unsigned and zero-extends; load byte signed (LDURSB) works with signed integers. C programs almost always use bytes for characters, not very short signed integers, so LDURB is used practically exclusively.
    Hardware/Software Interface (addresses): memory addresses naturally start at 0 and go to the largest address; negative addresses make no sense. Programs sometimes want numbers that can be positive or negative and sometimes only positive. C calls the former integers (long long int) and the latter unsigned integers (unsigned long long int). Some C style guides recommend signed long long int to keep the distinction clear.

    Shortcut 1: negation

    Invert every 0 to 1 and every 1 to 0, then add one. The sum of a number and its inverted representation must be 111…111two, which represents −1. Since x + x̄ = −1, therefore x + x̄ + 1 = 0, or x̄ + 1 = −x. (x̄ means invert every bit in x.)

    Example 2.4.2: 2ten = 00…0010. Invert: 11…1101. Add 1: 11…1110 = −2ten.
    Going back: 11…1110 inverted = 00…0001, plus 1 = 00…0010 = 2ten.

    Activity 2.4.8: if +3 is 00000011, then −3 in 8 bits is 11111101. 11111111…11111100two (64-bit) is −4.

    Shortcut 2: sign extension

    To convert an n-bit number to more than n bits, take the most significant bit (the sign bit) of the smaller quantity and replicate it to fill the new bits. The old nonsign bits are copied into the right portion. Animation 2.4.9: 2 in 32 bits (0000…0010) becomes 64 bits by copying the 0 sign bit. −2 in 32 bits (1111…1110) becomes 64 bits by copying the 1. It works because positive numbers really have infinitely many 0s on the left and negative numbers infinitely many 1s. The bit pattern hides leading bits to fit the hardware, and sign extension simply restores some of them.

    Elaborations

    • For signed decimal numbers we use “−” because decimal numbers have no size limit. With a fixed data size, binary and hexadecimal strings can encode the sign, so we don’t normally use “+” or “−” with binary or hex.
    • Two’s complement gets its name from the rule that the unsigned sum of an n-bit number and its n-bit negative is 2n, so the complement of x is 2n − x.
    • One’s complement: the negative is found by inverting each bit (x̄), and the complement of x is 2n − x − 1, which explains the name. It was also an attempt to beat sign and magnitude, and several early scientific computers used it. It is like two’s complement except it has two 0s: 00…00 is positive 0 and 11…11 is negative 0. The most negative number, 10…000two, is printed in the zyBook as −2,147,483,647ten, and the positives and negatives are balanced. One’s complement adders needed an extra step to subtract, so two’s complement dominates today.
    • Biased notation (floating point, Chapter 3): the most negative value is 00…000two, the most positive is 11…11two, and 0 typically is 10…00two. It biases the number so that the number plus the bias has a non-negative representation.

    One’s complement: a notation that represents the most negative value by 10…000two and the most positive value by 01…11two, leaving an equal number of negatives and positives but ending up with two zeros, one positive (00…00two) and one negative (11…11two).     Biased notation: a notation that represents the most negative value by 00…000two and the most positive value by 11…11two, with 0 typically having the value 10…00two, thereby biasing the number such that the number plus the bias has a non-negative representation.

    2.5 Representing instructions in the computer

    • Instructions are kept as a series of high and low electronic signals and may be represented as numbers. Each piece of an instruction is an individual number, and placing them side by side forms the instruction.
    • The 32 LEGv8 registers are referred to by number, 0 to 31.
    • A machine instruction is composed of fields, each several bits and representing part of the instruction.

    Animation 2.5.1, ADD X9, X20, X21, has 5 fields:

    111210001011000 · 11 bits
    2110101 · 5
    0000000 · 6

    The first field (1112) says the instruction performs addition. The second field is the register number of the second source operand (21 for X21), and the fourth field is the other source (20 for X20). The fifth field is the register receiving the sum (9 for X9). The third field is unused here, so it is 0.

    • This layout is the instruction format. The instruction takes exactly 32 bits, a word, or one half of a doubleword. By simplicity favors regularity, all LEGv8 instructions are 32 bits long.
    • The numeric version of instructions is machine language, and a sequence of them is machine code.
    Instruction format: a form of representation of an instruction composed of fields of binary numbers.
    Machine language: binary representation used for communication within a computer system.

    LEGv8 fields

    • opcode: basic operation of the instruction. This abbreviation is its traditional name.
    • Rm: the second register source operand.
    • shamt: shift amount (explained in 2.6; until then the field contains zero).
    • Rn: the first register source operand.
    • Rd: the register destination operand. It gets the result of the operation.

    A problem: load register must specify two registers and a constant. If the address used a 5-bit field, the largest constant would be only 25 − 1 or 31. The constant selects elements from arrays or data structures and often must be much larger, so a 5-bit field is too small. This creates a conflict between keeping all instructions the same length and having a single instruction format.

    Design Principle 3: Good design demands good compromises. (The final hardware design principle.) The LEGv8 compromise: keep all instructions the same length, which requires distinct formats for different kinds of instructions.
    • The format above is R-type (for register) or R-format.
    • D-type or D-format is used by data transfer instructions (loads and stores): opcode 11 | address 9 | op2 2 | Rn 5 | Rt 5.
    • The 9-bit address can load any doubleword within ±28 or 256 bytes (±25 or 32 doublewords) of the address in the base register Rn.
    • More than 32 registers would be difficult in this format, as Rn and Rt would each need another bit, making it harder to fit everything in one word.
    • The last D-type field is Rt instead of Rd because for stores it is a data source, not a destination.
    • LDUR X9, [X22,#64]: 22 goes in Rn, 64 in the address field, and 9 in Rt. In a load, Rt specifies the destination register, which receives the result. A destination register is a register that receives the result of an operation.
    • Immediate instructions (ADDI, SUBI, and others later) could have used the D-format’s 9-bit constant, but the ARMv8 architects wanted a larger immediate, even shaving a bit from the opcode to make a 12-bit immediate: I-type is opcode 10 | immediate 12 | Rn 5 | Rd 5.
    • Multiple formats complicate the hardware, but keeping them similar reduces complexity: the last two fields of all three formats are the same size and almost the same names, and the opcode field is the same size in two of the three.
    • Formats are distinguished by the first field (opcode): each format gets a distinct set of opcode values, so hardware knows how to treat the rest of the instruction.

    Big Picture: today’s computers are built on two key principles: (1) instructions are represented as numbers; (2) programs are stored in memory to be read or written, just like data. These lead to the stored-program concept, whose invention “let the computing genie out of its bottle.” Memory can hold an editor’s source code, its compiled machine code, the text the program is using, and even the compiler. Programs are often shipped as files of binary numbers, so computers can inherit ready-made software if compatible with an existing ISA. This “binary compatibility” leads industry to align around a small number of ISAs.

Treating instructions like data simplifies both memory hardware and software: the same memory technology works for programs, and compilers can translate human-friendly notation into machine code.

    2.6 Logical operations

    Opening quote: “Contrariwise,” continued Tweedledee, “if it was so, it might be; and if it were so, it would be; but as it isn’t, it ain’t. That’s logic.” The book attributes it to Lewis Carroll, Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland, 1865.

    • The first computers operated on full words, but it soon became clear it was useful to operate on fields of bits or individual bits. Example: examining characters within a word, each stored as 8 bits (section 2.9).
    • So operations were added to languages and ISAs to simplify, among other things, the packing and unpacking of bits into words. These are logical operations.

    Shifts

    • The first class is shifts: move all the bits in a doubleword left or right, filling the emptied bits with 0s.
    • Animation 2.6.1: shifting 9 left by 4 discards the leftmost 4 bits and shifts 4 zeros into the rightmost 4 bits, giving 144.
    • The dual of shift left is shift right. The actual names are logical shift left (LSL) and logical shift right (LSR): LSL X11, X19, #4 // reg X11 = reg X19 << 4 bits.
    • The delayed R-format field shamt stands for shift amount. LSL is encoded with 1691 in the opcode, Rd = 11, Rn = 19, shamt = 4, and Rm unused, so 0.
    • Bonus: shifting left by i bits is the same as multiplying by 2i, just as shifting a decimal number by i digits multiplies by 10i. Shifting 9 left by 4 is 9 × 24 = 9 × 16 = 144.

    Activity 2.6.2 (initial 00000011 00…00 00001111 00000001): shift right by 8 gives 00000000 00000011 00…00 00001111. Shift left by 8 gives 00…00 00001111 00000001 00000000. Shift left by 2 gives 00001100 00…00 00111100 00000100. Multiply by 2 gives 00000110 00…00 00011110 00000010 (same as shift left by 1).

    AND, OR, NOT, EOR

    • The book capitalizes AND to avoid confusion with the English conjunction. AND leaves a 1 only if both operand bits are 1.
    • Example: X10 = …00111100 00000000 and X11 = …00001101 11000000. AND X9, X10, X11 gives X9 = …00001100 00000000.
    • AND can apply a bit pattern to force 0s wherever the pattern has a 0. Such a pattern used with AND is traditionally called a mask, since it “conceals” some bits.
    • To place a value into one of these “seas of 0s,” use the dual of AND, OR: a 1 if either operand bit is 1. ORR X9, X10, X11 with the same registers gives …00111101 11000000.
    • The final logical operation is “a contrarian”: NOT takes one operand and inverts it, computing x̄.
    • To keep the three-operand format, ARMv8’s designers included EOR (Exclusive OR) instead of NOT. EOR gives 0 when bits are the same and 1 when they differ, so NOT is EOR with 111…111.
    • With X10 unchanged and X12 = 11…1111, EOR X9, X10, X12 gives 11111111 … 11000011 11111111. (The book’s comment on this line says “reg X10 | reg X12,” a typo for ^.)
    • Constants are useful in logical operations too, so LEGv8 has and immediate (ANDI), or immediate (ORRI), and exclusive or immediate (EORI).
    AND: a logical bit-by-bit operation with two operands that calculates a 1 only if there is a 1 in both operands.
    OR: a logical bit-by-bit operation with two operands that calculates a 1 if there is a 1 in either operand.
    NOT: a logical bit-by-bit operation with one operand that inverts the bits; that is, it replaces every 1 with a 0, and every 0 with a 1.
    EOR: a logical bit-by-bit operation with two operands that calculates the exclusive OR of the two operands. That is, it calculates a 1 only if the values are different in the two operands.

    Elaborations

    • C bit fields: C lets fields be defined within doublewords, both to pack objects in a doubleword and to match an externally enforced interface such as an I/O device. All fields must fit within a single doubleword. Fields are unsigned integers that can be as short as 1 bit. C compilers insert and extract fields with AND, ORR, LSL, and LSR.
    • ARMv8 logical immediates: the immediate fields of ANDI, ORRI, and EORI in full ARMv8 are not simple 12-bit immediates. Like ARMv7, ARMv8 uses a complex algorithm, with repeating patterns. Some small constants (1, 2, 3, 4, 6) are valid; others (−1, 0, 5) aren’t. LEGv8 simply uses normal 12-bit immediates as in ADDI, so EORI X1,X1,#5 is legal for LEGv8 but not ARMv8. The encoding is omitted because it is rare among instruction sets and would complicate the Chapter 4 datapath significantly.
    • Shifted-register operands: unlike almost all other architectures, ARMv8 (and ARMv7) allows a register to be shifted as part of an arithmetic or logical instruction (add an optionally shifted register, and so on). This is unusual, not frequently generated by compilers, and would make the Chapter 4 datapath much more complicated, so LEGv8 treats shifts as separate instructions, as in virtually every other architecture. You could synthesize a shift with ADD or OR with XZR, but using the same opcode would be confusing. So LEGv8 follows ARMv8’s recommendation of the UBFM (unsigned bitfield move) instruction and opcode. LEGv8 simplifies Rm and shamt to 0 and the shift amount. The actual UBFM values are (−shift amount MOD 64) and (63 − shift amount) for LSL, and shift amount and 63 for LSR. The opcode includes part of the immediate field in ARMv8, so LEGv8 makes the opcodes 1691 (LSL) and 1690 (LSR).

    2.7 Instructions for making decisions

    Opening quote, Burks, Goldstine, and von Neumann, 1947: the utility of an automatic computer lies in using a given sequence of instructions repeatedly, the number of iterations depending on the results of the computation. This choice can depend on the sign of a number (zero being reckoned as plus for machine purposes). So they introduce a conditional transfer instruction which, depending on a number’s sign, causes the proper one of two routines to be executed.
    • What distinguishes a computer from a simple calculator is its ability to make decisions: based on input data and computed values, different instructions execute.
    • Decision making is commonly represented with the if statement, sometimes combined with go to statements and labels. LEGv8 has two decision instructions similar to an if with a go to.
    • CBZ register, L1: go to the statement labeled L1 if the value in register equals zero. CBZ = compare and branch if zero.
    • CBNZ register, L1: go to L1 if the value in register does not equal zero. CBNZ = compare and branch if not zero.
    • These two are traditionally called conditional branches.
    Conditional branch: an instruction that tests a value and that allows for a subsequent transfer of control to a new address in the program based on the outcome of the test.

    If-then-else (Animation 2.7.1, COD Figure 2.9)

    if (i == j) f = g + h; else f = g - h;     // f..j in X19..X23
      SUB  X9, X22, X23     // X9 = i - j
      CBNZ X9, Else         // go to Else if i != j (X9 != 0)
      ADD  X19, X20, X21    // f = g + h (skipped if i != j)
      B    Exit             // go to Exit
Else: SUB  X19, X20, X21    // f = g - h (skipped if i == j)
Exit:
    • Example: if i is 9 and j is 9, the add executes.
    • If i and j are not equal, branch to the Else part, which needs a label. One might compare for equal instead, but testing for not equal yields more efficient code.
    • If i == j, the first part executes, and a jump to Exit skips the Else part. Either path ends at Exit.
    • The assembler relieves the compiler and programmer from calculating branch addresses, just as it does data addresses for loads and stores.
    Hardware/Software Interface: compilers frequently create branches and labels where they don’t appear in the programming language. Not having to write explicit labels and branches is one benefit of high-level languages and a reason coding is faster at that level.

    Decisions are important both for choosing between alternatives (if statements) and for iterating (loops). The same assembly instructions build both.

    Condition codes

    • Equality or inequality is probably the most popular test, but a for loop may need to test if an index is less than 0. The full set of comparisons: <, ≤, >, ≥, =, ≠.
    • Comparisons must deal with signed vs. unsigned. With signed numbers, a 1 in the MSB means negative, less than any positive. With unsigned, a 1 in the MSB means larger than any number starting with 0. (This dual meaning is used later to reduce the cost of array bounds checking.)
    • Architects handle all cases with four extra bits recording what happened during an instruction, called condition codes or flags:
      • negative (N): the result that set the condition code had a 1 in the MSB
      • zero (Z): the result was 0
      • overflow (V): the result overflowed
      • carry (C): the result had a carry out of the MSB or a borrow into the MSB
    • Conditional branches use combinations of these. The LEGv8 instruction is B.cond. Signed: EQ (=), NE (≠), LT (<, Less Than), LE (≤), GT (>), GE (≥). Unsigned: LO (<, Lower), LS (≤, Lower or Same), HI (>, Higher), HS (≥, Higher or Same).
    • Besides the 10 conditional branches in COD Figure 2.10, four more test individual condition code bits: B.MI (branch on minus, N=1), B.PL (branch on plus, N=0), B.VS (branch on overflow set, V=1), B.VC (branch on overflow clear, V=0).
    • Alternatives to condition codes: compare two registers and branch on the result, or compare two registers and set a third register to indicate success, which a later conditional branch tests for non-zero (true) or zero (false). MIPS follows the latter approach (section 2.16).

    Overflow

    • Recall: overflow occurs when the result can’t be represented with the available hardware, here a 64-bit word.
    • Adding operands with different signs can never overflow. The sum can be no larger than one of the operands. The book’s example: −10 + 4 = −6. Since the operands fit in 64 bits and the sum is no larger than an operand, the sum fits too.
    • Subtraction is the opposite: when the operands have the same sign, overflow can’t occur. That’s because c − a = c + (−a): subtracting same-signed operands turns into adding different-signed ones.
    • Adding or subtracting two 64-bit numbers can yield a result that needs 65 bits. Without a 65th bit, the sign bit is set with the value of the result instead of the proper sign. Since only one extra bit is needed, only the sign bit can be wrong.
    • Overflow in addition: two positives give a negative sum, or vice versa. This spurious sum means a carry out occurred into the sign bit.
    • Overflow in subtraction: positive − negative gives a negative, or negative − positive gives a positive. This “ridiculous result” means a borrow occurred from the sign bit.

    Elaborations

    • Saturating operations are a feature not generally found in general-purpose microprocessors. Saturation means that when a calculation overflows, the result is set to the largest positive number or the most negative number, instead of a modulo calculation as in two’s complement. It’s likely what you want for media operations. The book’s example is a radio volume knob: without saturation, turning it up would get louder for a while and then suddenly very soft. A saturating knob stops at the highest volume. Multimedia extensions to standard instruction sets often offer saturating arithmetic.
    • Faster addition: finding the carry into the high-order bits earlier increases speed. Various schemes anticipate the carry so the worst case is a function of the log2 of the number of bits in the adder. These signals are faster because they pass through fewer gates in sequence, but it takes many more gates to anticipate the carry. The most popular is carry lookahead (Appendix A, Section A.6).

    • The first operand is the multiplicand, the second the multiplier, and the result the product.
    • The grammar-school algorithm takes the multiplier digits one at a time from right to left, multiplies the multiplicand by that single digit, and shifts the intermediate product one digit to the left of the earlier ones. The intermediate products are then added.
    • Ignoring sign bits, an n-bit multiplicand times an m-bit multiplier needs n + m bits for all possible products. So multiply must cope with overflow too, since we often want a 64-bit product from two 64-bit numbers.
    • With only 0 and 1, each step is simple: place a copy of the multiplicand (1 × multiplicand) in the proper place if the multiplier digit is 1, or place 0 (0 × multiplicand) if it’s 0. Binary always offers only these two choices.

    First version (COD Figures 3.3 and 3.4)

    • The design mimics grammar-school multiplication, drawn so data flows top to bottom like the paper-and-pencil method.
    • Widths: Multiplicand register, ALU, and Product register are all 128 bits. Only the Multiplier register is 64 bits.
    • The multiplier is in the 64-bit Multiplier register, and the 128-bit Product register is initialized to 0. Over 64 steps, a 64-bit multiplicand moves 64 bits left, so the Multiplicand register is 128 bits, initialized with the multiplicand in the right half and zero in the left half.
    • The Control test checks Multiplier0 (the multiplier’s least significant bit) to decide when to shift the Multiplicand and Multiplier registers and when to write the Product register.
    Repeat 64 times (COD Figure 3.4):
 1.  Test Multiplier0
 1a. If Multiplier0 = 1: add Multiplicand to Product, place result in Product register
     (If Multiplier0 = 0: go straight to step 2)

    • The left shift in step 2 moves the intermediate operands left, as with paper and pencil. The right shift in step 3 gives the next multiplier bit to examine.
    • If each step took one clock cycle, this algorithm would take almost 200 clock cycles to multiply two 64-bit numbers (3 steps × 64).
    • Addition and subtraction may be anywhere from 5 to 100 times more popular than multiply, so in many applications multiply can take several clock cycles without much harm. But Amdahl’s Law reminds us that even a moderate frequency for a slow operation can limit performance.

    Refined version (COD Figure 3.5)

    • The algorithm is easily refined to take one clock cycle per step by doing operations in parallel: the multiplier and multiplicand are shifted while the multiplicand is added to the product if the multiplier bit is 1. The hardware just tests the right bit and gets the preshifted multiplicand.
    • It’s further optimized to halve the width of the adder and registers by noticing unused portions.
    • Now the Multiplicand register, ALU, and Multiplier register are all 64 bits, with only the Product register at 128 bits. The product is shifted right.
    • The separate Multiplier register disappears: the multiplier is placed in the right half of the Product register.
    • The Product register should really be 129 bits to hold the adder’s carry out, but it’s drawn as 128 to highlight the evolution from Figure 3.3.
    Hardware/Software Interface: replacing arithmetic by shifts also happens when multiplying by constants. Some compilers replace multiplies by short constants with a series of shifts and adds. Almost every compiler performs the strength reduction optimization of substituting a left shift for a multiply by a power of 2.

    • The easiest way is to convert the multiplier and multiplicand to positive numbers, remember their original signs, run the algorithm leaving the signs out, and negate the product only if the original signs disagree.

    Faster multiplication (COD Figure 3.7)

    • Moore’s Law has provided enough resources to build much faster multipliers. Whether the multiplicand is added is known at the start by looking at each of the 64 multiplier bits.
    • Faster multiply essentially provides one 64-bit adder for each multiplier bit. One input is the multiplicand ANDed with a multiplier bit, and the other is the output of a prior adder.
    • The straightforward approach connects adders in a stack 64 high. The alternative organizes the 64 additions in a parallel tree, so instead of 64 add times you wait just log2(64), or six, 64-bit add times.
    • Caption: instead of using a single 64-bit adder 63 times, the hardware “unrolls the loop” to use 63 adders and organizes them to minimize delay. The inputs are Mplier63·Mcand … Mplier0·Mcand, and the outputs are Product127 … Product0 (with Product95..32 labeled in the middle).
    • Multiply can go even faster than six add times because of carry save adders (Appendix A.6) and because such a design is easy to pipeline so many multiplies run at once (Chapter 4).

    Multiply in LEGv8

    • To produce a properly signed or unsigned 128-bit product, LEGv8 has three instructions: multiply (MUL), signed multiply high (SMULH), and unsigned multiply high (UMULH).
    • MUL gives the integer 64-bit product (the lower half). SMULH or UMULH give the upper 64 bits, chosen by the types of multiplier and multiplicand.

    Summary: multiplication hardware simply shifts and adds, derived from grammar-school paper and pencil. Compilers use shifts for multiplying by powers of 2. With much more hardware, the adds can run in parallel and much faster.

    Hardware/Software Interface: LEGv8 multiply instructions do not set the overflow condition code, so software must check whether the product fits in 64 bits. There’s no overflow if the upper 64 bits are 0 for UMULH or the replicated sign of the lower 64 bits for SMULH.

    Why mechanical computers are in museums

    • Parts aren’t small, so many parts means large and massive. Parts are manufactured individually (expensive in time and money) and need assembly. Part shape and size determine function, and parts touch as they compute, causing wear, so accuracy degrades with use. Maximum part speed is subsonic, so many-step algorithms are slow. Conclusion: we need a better moving part.
    • The electron is an excellent moving part: its mass is minuscule (moves at near light speed), it has charge (control its motion with an electric field), and its volume is negligible. So electronic computers can be fast, lightweight, and small. Still a concern: cost.
    • “Sad fact for Boilermakers”: when electron and proton charges were first recognized as different, some used the names Black and Gold. In 1747 Benjamin Franklin chose positive and negative.

    • Colossus, the first programmable, electronic, digital computer: designed and built in 1943 by British cryptographers at Bletchley Park to break the Lorenz rotor stream cipher. About 2,500 vacuum tubes (EF36 and 7AK7 pentodes) used the presence or absence of electron charge to control an independent stream of electrons, performing Boolean logic and counting. Adams photographed the recreation at The National Museum of Computing. Speaker note: electronics refers to the behavior and movement of electrons in a vacuum, gas, or solid.
    • Vacuum tube pros and cons: 1000-fold speed increase over mechanical (👍👍); about the same volume (😐); considerable electrical energy (😐); tubes fail (weaken or burn out) (👎). After WWII, AT&T Bell Labs, seeking to replace tubes in the telephone system, researched electron behavior in semiconducting germanium crystals.
    • First transistor, December 1947, at AT&T Bell Labs, using a high-quality germanium crystal Bell Labs grew after learning how from Purdue researchers. By December 2022 (the 75th anniversary) humans had made about 2×1022 transistors, the most manufactured device ever: “Homo sapiens is becoming Homo transistorus.”
    • Elements: today computers use elemental silicon (Si) crystals; Ge and alloys such as GaAs and SiN are used for some devices. Graphene, a 2D single-atomic-layer form of carbon, may become important soon. Device cost and performance depend on the elements chosen. (The periodic-table slide circles the column block around C, Si, Ge, Sn, groups 13–15.)

    Lecture: Representation

    Opening quote: “All of the books in the world contain no more information than is broadcast as video in a single large city in a single year. Not all bits have equal value.” — Carl Sagan.

    Weighted positional representation

    • Learning objective: explain the weighting polynomial for a weighted positional representation of any positive integer base.
    • “Number representations are innumerable”: there are stunningly many ways to represent numbers with bit strings. Want fast negation? “You can have it in a jiffy.” One circuit to add and subtract? “You got it.” Eliminate carry bits? “Can do.” Every pair of adjacent integers (|X − Y| = 1) differing in exactly one bit? “No problem” (that’s Gray code).
    • Unsigned integer, base 2, weighted positional: used for positive integers and C pointers. The number and weights of the positions fully define the scheme. MSB is the leftmost bit (or byte), LSB the rightmost. Example: 001011 = 0×25 + 0×24 + 1×23 + 0×22 + 1×21 + 1×20 = 11.
    • “Playing a weighting game”: the generalized form is ±dn−1×re(n−1) ± … ± d1×re(1) ± d0×re(0). Vary the coefficients dj, the radix r, the exponents e(j), and the sign of each term to get representations suited to different design goals.

    Sign magnitude

    • The sign is an unweighted left bit (0 = +, 1 = −). The remaining bits are the magnitude in weighted positional form. The total number of bits defines an instance.
    • Quirk: +0 and −0. In 8 bits: 00000000 = +0, 10000000 = −0. Speaker note: the comparison circuit must see these two as EQUAL, not GT or LT.
    • How a designer chooses a representation: ask how well it supports the desired computations, i.e., how well it meets the criteria. Sign magnitude is “used by pencils and paper everywhere.”
      • Quick and cheap: negation (invert the sign bit); |X| (reset/clear the sign bit to 0); converting to more bits, e.g., 32 to 64 (insert zero bits between the sign bit and the magnitude’s MSB).
      • Not quick and cheap: Compare(A, B) — because −0 = +0, a negative isn’t always less than a positive, so the comparison truth table is less regular and needs a more complex circuit. Addition and subtraction: the result-bit truth table has inputs sign(A), sign(B), and compare(A, B). “Yikes.”

    Two’s complement

    • Weighted, but the MSB weight is negative: −2n−1, +2n−2, …, +21, +20. 4-bit examples: 0010 = 0(−8)+0(4)+1(2)+0(1) = +2. 1110 = −8 + 4 + 2 = −2.
    • Characteristics: add and subtract results are always correct; only one bit string for zero; the MSB is not an actual sign bit position but can be interpreted as the sign — it’s an implicit sign bit; |max negative| = |max positive + 1|; invert A then add 1 to get −A.
    • Evaluation (√ = good, – = bad): negation is slower and more expensive than sign magnitude (–); one zero (√); leftmost bit serves as sign (√); add/subtract gives correct implicit sign bit (√) and correct magnitude (√); the add/subtract circuit is identical to the unsigned one (√√).
    • Speaker note, negation shortcut: starting from the least significant digit, scan left for the first 1 and invert all bits more significant than it. This is equivalent to finding where carry propagation stops when adding 1 to the inverted string.
    • Modern computers all use the circuit that works for both unsigned and two’s complement: they’re hardware-native data types.

    Changing representation size (casting)

    • Objectives: convert unsigned and two’s complement to larger bit strings; explain why converting to smaller ones is fraught.
    • Goal for cast(x) to more bits: the represented value must not change, so the original weighted positions must not change.
    • Unsigned: add leading zero bits. Two’s complement: copy the MSB into the added positions — sign extension.
    • Caution for compilers: hardware may provide only sign-extension wires, so the compiler must translate cast(unsigned_int) differently from cast(int). Caution for source code: casting to fewer bits can lose information.
    • Example: 2 + (−3) in 4 bits: 0010 + 1101 = 1111 with carry-out 0 (the slide labels bits C3 S3 S2 S1 S0). MSB = 1, so negative. Magnitude: (1111)′ + 1 = 0000 + 1 = 0001, so −1. In 8 bits: 00000010 + 11111101 = 11111111; the extended (green) bits show sign extension.
    • Speaker-note aside (end-around carry, as in one’s complement): 0010 + 1100 (+2 + −3) = 1110, but (−1 + −3 = −4) looks like 1110 + 1100 = 1010 with carry out, and adding the carry around gives 1010 + 1 = 1011 = −4.

    Arithmetic with k-bit strings

    • A k-bit integer representation is an element of ℤk = {0, 1, …, 2k−1}, the nonnegative integers less than 2k. (The slide labels the set ℤk although its modulus is 2k.)
    • A k-bit and a (k+1)-bit integer are different data types. So a result needn’t be cast before becoming a future input, a k-bit function circuit typically has k-bit inputs and a k-bit output.
    • Adding two k-bit unsigned integers can produce a (k+1)-bit result, output as the k least significant bits because that’s all the wires there are. A result too large for the representation is overflow. The actual sum 1xk−1…x0 maps to 0xk−1…x0: Output = Actual_Sum (mod 2k).
    • Arithmetic modulo k: A +k B = (A + B) mod k; A ·k B = (A · B) mod k. They satisfy closure, associativity, commutativity, distributivity of ·k over +k, identity elements, and additive inverses. Multiplicative inverses don’t always exist; it depends on k and the element.

    Designing with bit strings: Gray code

    • Bit strings are a technology for representation. Representations are designed all the time (e.g., defining a struct, a collection of native types). Design should be intentional: satisfy requirements and goals.
    • Example goal: encode rotational position of a gear unambiguously. Positions 0 (top), 1 (right), 2 (bottom), 3 (left). With 2-bit unsigned, rotating clockwise from 3 to 0 goes 11 → 00.
    • Glitch: voltage transitions on two wires aren’t truly simultaneous to a very fast circuit, so 3 → 0 may be sensed as 11 → 10 → 00 or 11 → 01 → 00, i.e., positions 3, 2, 0 or 3, 1, 0.
    • Solution: a modular integer representation that changes by only one bit between adjacent angles: Gray code, used for angular position sensors. Adjacent (mod) codes differ in only one bit.

    • Design involves invention; a design process produces designs; designs are compared using criteria (plural of criterion).

    Text representation

    • Objectives: relate hardware cost and character set design; explain key design aspects of ASCII.
    • Baudot Code (1870): 5 bits/char, so 25 = 32 chars: Roman letters, punctuation, control signals. Typed on a 5-piano-key keyboard using two left-hand and three right-hand fingers. Encodings were chosen to minimize keyboard operator fatigue [a design criterion]. It became International Telegraph Alphabet No. 1. (Photo: Science Museum Group, Baudot five-key keyboard 1897–1926.)
    • 1901, Donald Murray modified Baudot, introducing CR (carriage return) and LF (line feed) to support typewriting on 2D paper. Before, human operators converted received code to handwriting and did the formatting themselves. CR and LF let teletype machines (tele = at a distance; type = write) be programmed to start new lines. Used by teletypes until the 1950s. Speaker-note pun: tele-vision, tele-phone, and at Starfleet Academy you move your bags by “tele-porter.”
    • ASCII (1963), American Standard Code for Information Interchange: 7-bit, 128 characters. Adds digits 0–9, both letter cases, and many new control characters. Encoding was redesigned for machine-oriented criteria rather than human ones:
      • easier machine sorting: alphabetic_order = (int) ASCII_char_code;
      • simpler case-insensitive matching and keyboard/printer logic: upper and lower case differ in only one bit (decide case by examining 1 bit).
    • The ASCII chart (textbook Figure 3.6) highlights 07 bel, 0A lf, 41 A, and 61 a: upper and lower case intentionally differ by 0x20; 07 and 0A are examples of control characters. Other anchors: 20 = space, 30 = ‘0’, 7F = del.
    • A teletype terminal with paper tape punch and reader (keyboard and “screen” functionality, no computing) cost about as much as a new automobile that same year. Speaker notes: character sets contain traces of ancient computing history; Adams first sent a program to a computer on a Teletype the summer after his junior year of high school. We call moving through text “scrolling” because the first computer display was a big roll of paper.
    • Unicode: managed by the Unicode Consortium; an outgrowth of 1987 work by Xerox and Apple; up to 4 bytes per character. Version 17.0 is current as of Sep. 9, 2025. It added 4,803 characters and 4 scripts (Sidetic, Tolong Siki, Beria Erfe, Tai Yo) to version 16.0 (Sep. 2024), for a total of 172 scripts, 159,801 characters, and 3,790 emoji.
    • Programming spread in the teletype era with limited ASCII, so most languages are written in ASCII: >= and != exist because ASCII has no ≥ or ≠, and ➗ generally isn’t recognized by compilers.

    Byte order (endianness)

    • Software most often addresses memory by the byte. Bytes of a byte array are numbered first to last; bytes of an 8-byte integer might be numbered least to most significant. Designs need only be internally consistent. Speaker note: internal wiring doesn’t affect the programmer.
    • But byte order of multi-byte objects is exposed to software in memory, and when sending multi-byte data across a network the order must be agreed on to avoid scrambling. Byte order is visible and important.
    • Imagine reading memory from lowest to highest address. Big Endian: the most significant (“big”) byte is at the lowest address, then MSB-to-LSB order. Little Endian: the reverse; the least significant (“little”) byte is at the lowest address.
    • Example 0x00C0F380. Little endian at addresses 0–3: 80, F3, C0, 00. Big endian: 00, C0, F3, 80. The order of bits within a byte is NEVER changed.

    Lecture: Instruction encoding

    Opening quote: “I started out with machine code and assembly language.” — Charles Petzold, Microsoft MVP.

    What operations should a processor offer?

    • The CPU is general purpose. Choosing a general-purpose set of machine instructions is hard and strongly affected by the available technology. Objectives: list at least four tradeoffs ISA designers face; explain the three parts of a machine instruction.
    • Instruction representations are named by processor family. Historic: PDP-1, IBM 360, DEC VAX, x86. Current: SPARC, x86-64 (aka AMD64, x86_64, x64, Intel 64), Power, ARM (v7, v8).
    • Choosing the operations is hard because criteria sets are large, members change with time, member weights change with time, and weight considerations — technology, manufacturability, marketplace — are in tension. The chosen set is the instruction set architecture (ISA).
    • HLL vs. machine ops: an HLL may have an operation with no single machine instruction (e.g., % remainder); a machine may have an instruction matching no HLL operation (e.g., DEC VAX has solve for roots of a 31st-order polynomial). What’s necessary is that every HLL operation is possible via some composition of machine instructions.
    • ISA considerations: the ability to execute every possible program, Turing completeness, requires little of an ISA. Beyond that minimum, tradeoffs among programmer convenience (a few lines doing significant computation), processor cost, engineering considerations, heat created in the circuit, and speed of program execution.
    • ISA purpose: to control the general-purpose processor circuit. A bit string paired with an instruction representation must configure the circuit to execute a specific instruction.

    Instruction fields

    • Instructions are bit strings; design the format (textbook Figure 5.1: opcode | operand 1 | operand 2 | … — “the general instruction format that many processors use. The opcode at the beginning determines exactly which operands follow”). Figure 5.1 omits a field for Results (see chapter 5.4). Speaker note: omitting it is not exactly an error, because of implicit addressing.
    • Opcode: selects which ALU result to use. Required because the processor must be told what to do. Encoding is the designer’s choice, because machine instructions aren’t intended to be portable and decoding costs time and money.
    • Operands 1, 2, …: provide input to the ALU. Optional (the opcode says how many). Encoded as a pointer to the location storing the operand, or specially as the value itself (e.g., a two’s complement immediate).
    • Result(s): must be a pointer to storage location(s). Optional. Not rare: a zero-bit pointer for the result (implicit).

    Instruction size: variable vs. fixed

    • Objectives: pros/cons of variable and fixed length; how growth in memory locations challenges ISAs; how a register unit solves it for fixed length.
    • Both are quantized in bytes. Variable: concatenate fields with no limit on number or sizes. Fixed: encode every instruction in a fixed number of bytes.
    • Variable length advantages: unlimited ISA capability (“just add bytes”); short encodings for common instructions reduce program size. Disadvantages: hard to prevent Marketing from adding to the ISA; decoding is complex (“How many bytes to examine? OK, solved that, now, what do the bytes say?”); variable anything makes hardware slower. Today compiler writers care some about ISA capability; few other programmers code in machine language.
    • Fixed length advantages: easy to fetch a fixed-size object quickly; few representations means small chip area and energy for decoding; ISA criteria are largely technical because you can’t add bytes; compiler writers welcome a smaller ISA (less time, better quality compiling). Disadvantages: instructions may not use all bits, so machine code is slightly larger than variable length; no Marketing messages about “expanded ISA.”

    x86: the oldest ISA in wide use today

    • Intel 4004, the first commercial microprocessor (1971–1981): 2,300 PMOS transistors; native 4-bit BCD digit; 16-pin DIP with a 4-bit I/O bus, 4-bit address_out bus, ROM control, reset, sync, test, clock (2 pins), +V, and ground.
    • Hardware criteria then: memory was expensive and a location stored one byte; chip packages had few pins (limited I/O). So: keep programs small, use 8-bit instructions with special-purpose registers such as an accumulator. Naming a pre-selected register needs 0 bits, keeping instructions short.
    • Software criteria then: programmers often wrote machine language, so include sophisticated instructions [future problem: compilers can’t readily map HLL code to complex instructions]. Strategy: extend encodings in 8-bit increments to add instructions; increasing decode complexity accepted as a necessary cost.
    • Intel 8080, second 8-bit design (1974–1990): 6,000 NMOS transistors, easier to interface; 40-pin DIP; 16-bit address; −5 V, +5 V, +12 V power. Target for the CP/M OS. Marketplace success and the value of existing software made binary compatibility required for follow-ons such as the 8086: the start of x86. The Intel 8088 was in the original IBM PC.

    What drives up instruction length?

    • Opcode field? No. Since 2k × 2 = 2k+1, doubling the number of operations adds only 1 bit.
    • Operand and result fields? Yes! Three memory pointer fields; memory locations are typically 1 byte. So 16 GB needs log2(16 × 230) = log2(24+30) = 34-bit pointers. 128 GB → 37 bits each.
    • Growing pointer size obsoletes machines: memory has become dramatically cheaper (Moore’s Law), and software value tends to increase as bytes of instructions and data increase. Result: general-purpose hardware with pointers ≤ 32 bits is obsolete. Variable-length ISAs adapt by “address extension” and more bytes per instruction.
    • Fixed-length strategy: general-purpose registers fix pointer size (“fix” = make it a constant). Limit instruction pointers to a small number of processor registers; when a memory pointer is needed, have the processor compute it. Memory can grow while in-instruction pointers don’t.
    • So ALU instructions point only to registers in the register file (register unit); LOAD and STORE compute a pointer to memory. To add two integers: LOAD operands into registers, ALU op into a register, STORE the result.

    Pointing to memory locations

    Opening quote: “I had amnesia once or twice.” — Steven Wright.

    • Memory’s required functions: write (store a bit string) and read (load, by a memory-using device), over an input/output bus.
    • Memory contains locations. Reads and writes happen at one location at a time (cheap, easy). Locations must be individually identifiable: naming, addressing, or pointing. {naming, addressing, pointing} is a function mapping a {name, address, pointer value} to one location.
    • Abstract diagram: a k-bit pointer goes into a “pointer to wire” mapping circuit that drives 2k wires, one to each of 2k locations (Location 00…0000 through FF…FFFF).
    • Functions mapping bit strings to bit strings can be defined by a truth table: inputs = the domain bit string, outputs = the range bit string. Examples built in class: f(A) = A′ (NOT); f(A, B) = A AND B; f(A, B, Cin) = Sum and g(A, B, Cin) = Cout. Speaker-note example with 4-bit operands: 01110 (14) + 01100 (12) = 11010 (26).
    • k-bit pointing truth table: convert k wires carrying unsigned integer P into 2k wires with only wire P asserted. Name address inputs A0 … Ak−1 and location outputs L0 … L(2k−1). Start with k = 2, then 3. Speaker notes: adding 1 input bit doubles the number of rows (addresses, locations). “When processor designers select instructions for their ISA, they create the truth table for the circuit for each operation.”
    • Optimized for chips: memory chips are 2D, so use a grid of wires as coordinates. A square array of 2k/2 horizontal and 2k/2 vertical wires makes 2k intersections with only 2 × 2k/2 = 2(1+k/2) wires. That’s much less than 2k for, say, k > 16 (more than 64 K locations), so it uses chip area well and costs less. Speaker note: one reason to be comfortable with exponents in powers of 2.

    Decoders, buses, mux and demux

    • Objectives: design the truth table and circuit for an active-high or active-low decoder of any size; explain the inputs, outputs, and control inputs of multiplexers and demultiplexers and the decoder’s role in both. Vocabulary: decoder, bus, mux/demux.
    • To read or write memory you must first point at a location (select it alone). Then either transport a copy of its bit string to a destination (read) or send a bit string to it (write/store).
    • Decoder: a memory address is an “encoded” location. Input: n wires encoding address A from 2n addresses. Output: 2n wires, one per address. Only the wire for A asserts; all others take the non-asserting voltage. Each output tells its location “selected” or “not_selected.” Example: a 2-to-4 decoder with inputs XY and outputs D0–D3. At any time, only one output is asserted.
    • Bus: a group of N wires carrying N bits simultaneously.
    • Multiplexer (mux): selects 1 of 2n k-bit input buses to connect to the k-bit output bus. Used to read (copy) from a memory location. Demultiplexer (demux): connects the k-bit input bus to 1 of 2n output buses (the reverse). Used to write.
    • Diagrams get more abstract: a box with 32-bit input buses 0 … 2n−1 and n address bits; then the textbook’s mux symbol (e.g., a 2-input mux of unspecified width) omits the address wires because (1) they must be there and (2) their number is always log2(# input buses).
    • 1-of-8 mux: 8 k-bit buses from 8 locations; output bus A. 23 k-bit switches; a selected switch joins its B bus to the A bus. A 3×8 decoder with Enable controls which switch is selected from 3 address bits. The slide’s logic diagram is TI’s SN74CB3Q3251 “1-of-8 FET multiplexer/demultiplexer, 2.5-V/3.3-V low-voltage high-bandwidth bus switch” (select inputs S0, S1, S2 and an active-low output enable, OE̅).
    • With bidirectional buses the same part is a mux/demux: B1–B8 are inputs for mux and outputs for demux; bus A is the mux output and demux input.
    • Summary: transporting a bit string from one of many storage locations to a processor circuit is a fetch. To fetch a sequence of instructions, input a sequence of addresses to a mux connected to those locations’ buses.
    Vocabulary slide: Full adder – 1-bit integer addition circuit. Decoder – logic circuit for pointing. Multiplexer (mux) – selects one data bus from many and connects it to one output bus. Demultiplexer (demux) – connects one input bus to one of many output buses. Buses do not cast except intentionally, e.g., sign extension.

    Lecture: Program characteristics

    Control structures and the execution trace

    • Objectives: define the execution trace; describe the trace of the three structures used to define any algorithm; understand the most common location of the next instruction; understand that the trace embodies the program’s control flow.
    • An HLL source file (listing) is a sequence of statements — a one-dimensional array of statements. The algorithm is defined by scanning from first line to last.
    • A machine program is a sequence of machine instructions stored consecutively in listing order by increasing byte addresses. Instruction boundaries fall at a variable interval for variable-length ISAs and a fixed interval (an array data structure) for fixed-length ones.
    • Fetch–Execute algorithm, performed while energy is supplied: Repeat forever { FETCH – access the next program instruction from where it is stored. EXECUTE – perform the action(s) described by the instruction. } Speaker note: simple single-purpose computers often have no HALT instruction — Apple TV, a microwave oven controller — so to stop them you remove the power source.
    • Fetch means read (copy) an instruction from instruction memory. Pointing initiates fetch, so execution is controlled by the sequence of pointer values sent to memory: the execution trace.
    • Execute performs the computation and creates results. An execute step may create the pointer for the next fetch, so the trace can depend on results.
    • Three structures are sufficient to embody the logic of any algorithm: sequence, selection, repetition.

    Branches

    • Fetch the current instruction with the pointer in the Program Counter (PC) register. Fetch next at the default address (fetch++) or a different one. A branch instruction gives the choice: condition True → fetch at Branch_target; False → fetch at Default_next.
    • Branch target address = PC + Offset. Definition: if (Evaluate_expression) { PC ← PC + Offset } else { PC ← PC + sizeof(instruction) }. The target is unchanging, so Offset is constant and goes in an immediate field.
    • Speaker note: branches are a big use of the Offset field; the other big use is accessing array elements. “A program is an array of type instruction (plus any initialized data).”
    • Manual offset: the programmer computes Offset = bytes between the branch’s first byte and the target’s first byte, and writes it as the branch argument. Problems: (1) an error breaks the program; (2) inserting or deleting an instruction between branch and target means revising the offset — and every branch with that target; (3) “programmer productivity and happiness decline.”
    • Abstraction to the rescue: machine code has no abstraction (offset is a value, not a symbol). Assembly offers some: operation mnemonics (ASCII names), register names (r5 instead of 00101), and labels — a symbolic name for a memory address bit string. The programmer gives each target a unique label and writes the label instead of the offset.

    Two-pass assembler (textbook Figure 9.12)

    • Objectives: why 2 passes are necessary; how macros make keyboarding easier and coding harder.
    • The assembler determines each label’s address relative to an assumed 0x0 address for the first instruction, stores label–address pairs in the symbol table, computes Offset = Label_address − branch_instr_address, and puts it in the branch’s immediate field.
    • Figure 9.12 (“a snippet of assembly language code and the locations assigned to each statement for a hypothetical processor; locations are determined in the assembler’s first pass”): x: .long at 0x00; label1: cmp r1, r2 at 0x04; bne label2 at 0x08; jsr label3 at 0x0C; label2: load r3, 0 at 0x10; br label4 at 0x14; label3: add r5, 1 at 0x18; ret at 0x1C; label4: ld r1, 1 at 0x20; ret at 0x24.
    • Symbol table: x = 0x00, label1 = 0x04, label2 = 0x10, label3 = 0x18, label4 = 0x20. Offsets: bne label2 → 0x10 − 0x08 = 0x08; jsr label3 → 0x18 − 0x0C = 0x0C; br label4 → 0x20 − 0x14 = 0x0C.
    • Labels can’t always be defined before use: a forward branch target’s distance is unknown when first seen. The symbol is undefined when first encountered. Speaker note: it could have been called the “label table,” but maybe that sounded too silly — “CS is Computer SCIENCE, not Computer Silliness.”
    • Pass 1 stores each label and its relative address in the symbol table. Pass 2 builds machine code: replaces mnemonics, register names, etc. with bit strings in the instruction fields, and uses the symbol table to fill offsets (Offset = Label_relative_addr − Branch_instr_relative_addr).
    • Why only 1 pass for a compiler? Two passes make forward (undefined) references not an error and let programmers declare/define before or after use. But compilation is computationally intensive, and declaring/defining first is more readable and not much harder. 1-pass is a design choice for faster compiles and greater readability.
    • Assembly macros: “replace the tedium of repetitive keyboarding with the excitement of debugging macro errors.” Macros add one more pass: replace each use with the definition (expand) and substitutions. Syntax errors are found only during subsequent passes over the expanded code.

    Lab 03 concepts (you’ve completed this lab)

    • W vs. X registers: w0 is the low 32 bits of x0. An int uses w registers (4 bytes); a long uses x registers (8 bytes).
    • Brackets mean memory: str w0, [sp, 12] writes w0 to main memory (DRAM) at address sp + 12, using base + offset addressing. It writes 4 bytes, little endian.
    • Stack frames: sub sp, sp, #N allocates space first (like malloc before use), and add sp, sp, #N frees it. ARMv8 keeps sp 16-byte aligned, so five ints (20 bytes) get a 32-byte frame.
    • Return values go in w0/x0 (eax on x86-64). Falling off the end of main returns 0.
    • Load-store: g = 1; becomes mov then str, because a constant can’t go straight into memory. Declarations compile to no instructions; they become stack offsets and the frame size.
    • Size: ARM64 is 4 bytes per instruction. x86-64 is 1–15 bytes; your unoptimized main was 63 bytes.
    • -O: an unused result is deleted (dead code elimination, giving xor eax, eax / ret). A returned constant expression is computed at compile time (constant folding, giving mov eax, 2).
